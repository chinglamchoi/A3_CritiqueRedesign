Assignment 3 · Critique & Redesign

Reading the Spiral: A Redesign of the NYT COVID-19 Chart

The "tapeworm of doom" makes a striking image of the Omicron wave, but its visual rhetoric contradicts the optimistic forecast it was published to support. My redesign tries to recover both comparability and narrative honesty.

Vis & Society 2026 Ching Lam Choi Spring 2026

I · The Original

What the spiral shows

On January 6, 2022, at the peak of the Omicron wave, The New York Times Opinion section ran a guest essay by Dr. Jeffrey Shaman, a Columbia epidemiologist whose team built one of the first COVID-19 models. He argued that the United States would record more cases that month than in any prior month of the pandemic, but that a smaller fraction of those cases would become severe. The article led with the spiral chart pictured above, designed by Gus Wezerek and Sara Chodosh.

What can be read off the chart

  • Three discrete waves, each larger than the last. The spiral has three pronounced swells — summer 2020, winter 2020–21, and the January 2022 Omicron spike — and they escalate in visible thickness.
  • Omicron breaks the frame. The January 2022 plume bursts vertically off the canvas, far beyond any earlier band. This is the chart's unmistakable lead and the rhetorical reason it exists.
  • Major peaks cluster at the top of the spiral: winter. Both non-Omicron peaks fall near January, suggesting a seasonal pattern. (One does wonder how much of this clustering is real and how much is manufactured by the angular layout.)
  • A pronounced summer 2020 hump sits at the bottom of the inner ring, around July 2020: the smallest of the named waves but clearly visible.
  • The first months of 2020 are nearly invisible. The innermost coil shows almost no width: cases were either genuinely low or, more likely, severely undercounted in early pandemic reporting.
  • The chart ends in mid-flight. The outer band tapers but does not close — a deliberate signal that the Omicron wave was still unfolding at publication.

II · Critique

What works, and what doesn't

The chart's strongest move is rhetorical alignment with the moment. The January 2022 Omicron plume bursts vertically off the canvas — a visceral break in an otherwise contained shape — and that single gesture communicates "this wave is different" before a reader has parsed any axis. The clockwise angular encoding leverages a calendrical convention readers genuinely hold (months as positions on a circle), and the spiral's continuity embeds the wave in a longer pandemic timeline the reader already shares as lived memory. As an op-ed image, it earns the pause its place on the page demands.

The chart's central design choice, however, is in tension with the argument the article is making. Comparing magnitudes between waves requires reading band thickness on a curving baseline: position, the strongest visual channel, is converted into something much weaker. The question Shaman's piece depends on — "how does Omicron compare to last winter?" — is not answerable in a glance. The metaphorical register compounds the problem: a tightening, ever-expanding spiral connotes escalation without end, exactly the opposite of Shaman's actually-optimistic forecast that the wave will peak and recede within weeks. The chart visually shouts we are spiralling out while the prose argues the opposite. Smaller issues compound this: no entry point, no quantitative axis behind the legend's "150K cases" tick, and no sign of hospitalizations or deaths — which Shaman explicitly names as the actually-relevant burden. The redesign opportunity is not to strip the drama the op-ed earned, but to recover two things: comparability across waves, and narrative honesty between the visual gestalt and the article's conclusion.

III · Sketches

Three directions

The critique gives the redesign two charges: recover comparability across waves, and recover narrative honesty between the visual gestalt and Shaman's actually-optimistic argument. The three sketches below take deliberately different routes: one foregrounding seasonal rhythm, one foregrounding the cases-versus-severity decoupling, and one trying to do both at once.

SKETCH 01

Four Winters — annotated ridgeline by year

Motivation — recover the spiral's seasonal reading on a layout where waves are directly comparable, with each year a separate shape on a shared baseline. Hoped communication — year-on-year comparisons answerable at a glance (was Delta bigger than winter 2020–21?), with variant labels riding on the relevant ridges so the narrative sits inside the chart. Worked / didn't — comparability and seasonality both read clearly, but the chart loses the spiral's "one continuous pandemic" gestalt, and cases remain the only encoded variable. To explore next — cases alone aren't the article's argument; the next sketch should put a second variable on the page and see what happens to the visual rhythm.

SKETCH 02

The Decoupling — cases, hospitalizations, and deaths, rescaled

Motivation — pull Shaman's actual argument — the ratio of cases to severe outcomes — onto the page directly, with three series sharing a horizontal timeline, each rescaled to its own peak so all are visible together. Hoped communication — the visible gap that opens between the case curve and the death curve in late 2021 (when vaccines arrived and Omicron began) is itself the takeaway; vaccine and variant markers carry the why. Worked / didn't — foregrounds the article's scientific claim, which Sketch 01 misses entirely, but rescaling sacrifices absolute magnitudes: readers can compare shapes but not numbers, and the "Omicron broke the scale" feeling is gone. To explore next — can a single chart preserve seasonal pattern, show magnitude honestly, and hint at the severity story? A calendar-style heatmap with a second-variable strip might carry that load.

SKETCH 03

Pandemic Calendar — heatmap grid with a severity strip

Motivation — try to do both at once: rows = years, columns = weeks, cell color encoding case magnitude on a single shared scale, and a thin strip across the top encoding the severity ratio. Hoped communication — three readings in one image — when waves happened, how big, and how lethal — with the visual punchline being the Omicron block, densely colored cells beneath a conspicuously low severity line. Worked / didn't — compact, dense, still visually distinctive; not a chart type the average op-ed reader sees every week, so it earns its space the way the spiral did; the cost is that color is a coarser channel than position, so readers walk away with patterns rather than numbers. To carry into the final — this is the candidate to develop; open questions about per-capita normalization, the exact severity proxy, and color-scale calibration are deferred to the rationale in Section V.

IV · Final Design

Two Pandemics in One

V · Rationale & Reflection

Design rationale

Rationale & encoding choices

The redesign argues a single claim: the United States lived through two pandemics — one defined by case counts, one by lethality — that peaked a year apart. The mark is a calendar heatmap with a layered severity strip. Years occupy three rows and weeks occupy fifty-three columns of position reserved for the comparisons that matter most: when, and which year. Cell color encodes case magnitude, where pattern is enough. The case-fatality ratio sits on the strip in position-on-y, giving the article's central scientific claim its strongest possible channel. Daily counts are aggregated to ISO weeks to suppress noise; the color scale is square-root rather than linear because cases span four orders of magnitude; the CFR is computed with a two-week lag to honor the epidemiological gap between infection and death; and early 2020 is dropped from the strip because testing was too sparse to be meaningful. The colormap deliberately avoids red, an alarm color that would import the spiral's panicked register into a chart making an optimistic argument, and is anchored at the page's cream so empty cells disappear. What this design loses: the spiral's iconic lead-image quality. It is a reading chart, not a seeing chart; the smaller 2020 waves read paler than they felt at the time; and the CFR is itself a flawed severity proxy.

Reflection: critique by redesign

The redesign substantially recovers comparability: the question was Delta bigger than winter 2020-21?, unanswerable from the spiral, is now a comparison of two columns of differently-saturated blue; and partially recovers narrative honesty, with a calmer gestalt and a severity strip that carries Shaman's actual claim. But "critique by redesign" surfaced a tension the initial critique didn't quite see: a single chart cannot hold both visceral op-ed impact and analytical comparability. The spiral's drama was load-bearing for its place on a Sunday opinion page; a reader scrolling past would stop at the spiral, and will not, in the same way, stop at a calendar heatmap. The redesign chose comparability and accepts the loss. A fully realized op-ed version might honestly need both charts — the spiral as visceral lead, the calendar as body figure. The deeper lesson is that pandemic data resists single-chart treatment because it tracks several phenomena at once, moving at different paces. An honest critique should always specify which argument it is critiquing the design against; a redesign is not "better" in the abstract, only better at a specific argument.
