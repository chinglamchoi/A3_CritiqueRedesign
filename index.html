Assignment 3 · Critique & Redesign

Reading the Spiral: A Redesign of the NYT COVID-19 Chart

The "tapeworm of doom" makes a striking image of the Omicron wave, but its visual rhetoric contradicts the optimistic forecast it was published to support. My redesign tries to recover both comparability and narrative honesty.

Vis & Society 2026 Ching Lam Choi Spring 2026

I · The Original

What the spiral shows

On January 6, 2022, at the peak of the Omicron wave, The New York Times Opinion section ran a guest essay by Dr. Jeffrey Shaman, a Columbia epidemiologist whose team built one of the first COVID-19 models. He argued that the United States would record more cases that month than in any prior month of the pandemic, but that a smaller fraction of those cases would become severe. The article led with the spiral chart pictured above, designed by Gus Wezerek and Sara Chodosh.

What can be read off the chart

  • Three discrete waves, each larger than the last. The spiral has three pronounced swells — summer 2020, winter 2020–21, and the January 2022 Omicron spike — and they escalate in visible thickness.
  • Omicron breaks the frame. The January 2022 plume bursts vertically off the canvas, far beyond any earlier band. This is the chart's unmistakable lead and the rhetorical reason it exists.
  • Major peaks cluster at the top of the spiral: winter. Both non-Omicron peaks fall near January, suggesting a seasonal pattern. (One does wonder how much of this clustering is real and how much is manufactured by the angular layout.)
  • A pronounced summer 2020 hump sits at the bottom of the inner ring, around July 2020: the smallest of the named waves but clearly visible.
  • The first months of 2020 are nearly invisible. The innermost coil shows almost no width: cases were either genuinely low or, more likely, severely undercounted in early pandemic reporting.
  • The chart ends in mid-flight. The outer band tapers but does not close — a deliberate signal that the Omicron wave was still unfolding at publication.

II · Critique

What works, and what doesn't

The chart’s strongest move is how it aligns with the moment. The January 2022 Omicron wave rises sharply compared to earlier patterns, and this visual contrast signals that “this wave is different” even before the reader engages with the axes. The circular layout also draws on a familiar calendrical structure (months arranged around a year), and the continuous spiral situates the wave within the broader timeline of the pandemic. As an op-ed image, it is effective in capturing attention and conveying urgency.

However, this design sits somewhat uneasily with the article’s argument. Comparing magnitudes between waves is difficult because values are encoded through band thickness along a curved path, rather than position, which is generally easier to interpret. This makes it harder to answer the key question of how Omicron compares to previous waves at a glance. The spiral form may also suggest ongoing escalation, which does not fully align with the article’s more measured claim that the wave will peak and decline relatively quickly. Additional limitations—such as the lack of a clear entry point, limited quantitative reference points, and the absence of hospitalisation or death data—further constrain interpretation. A revision might retain the visual impact while improving comparability and better aligning the chart with the article’s central message.

III · Sketches

Three directions

The critique gives the redesign two main aims: to improve comparability across waves, and to better align the visual impression with Shaman’s cautiously optimistic argument. The three sketches below explore these aims in different ways: one foregrounds seasonal rhythm, one foregrounds the decoupling between cases and severity, and one attempts to hold both ideas together.

SKETCH 01

Four Winters — annotated ridgeline by year

Motivation — recover the spiral's seasonal reading on a layout where waves are directly comparable, with each year a separate shape on a shared baseline. Communication goal — year-on-year comparisons answerable at a glance (was Delta bigger than winter 2020–21?), with variant labels riding on the relevant ridges so the narrative sits inside the chart. Worked / didn't — comparability and seasonality both read clearly, but the chart loses the spiral's "one continuous pandemic" gestalt, and cases remain the only encoded variable. To explore next — cases alone aren't the article's argument; the next sketch should put a second variable on the page and see what happens to the visual rhythm.

SKETCH 02

The Decoupling — cases, hospitalizations, and deaths, rescaled

Motivation — pull Shaman's actual argument — the ratio of cases to severe outcomes — onto the page directly, with three series sharing a horizontal timeline, each rescaled to its own peak so all are visible together. Communication goal — the visible gap that opens between the case curve and the death curve in late 2021 (when vaccines arrived and Omicron began) is itself the takeaway; vaccine and variant markers carry the why. Worked / didn't — foregrounds the article's scientific claim, which Sketch 01 misses entirely, but rescaling sacrifices absolute magnitudes: readers can compare shapes but not numbers, and the "Omicron broke the scale" feeling is gone. To explore next — can a single chart preserve seasonal pattern, show magnitude honestly, and hint at the severity story? A calendar-style heatmap with a second-variable strip might carry that load.

SKETCH 03

Pandemic Calendar — heatmap grid with a severity strip

Motivation — try to do both at once: rows = years, columns = weeks, cell color encoding case magnitude on a single shared scale, and a thin strip across the top encoding the severity ratio. Communication goal — three readings in one image — when waves happened, how big, and how lethal — with the visual punchline being the Omicron block, densely colored cells beneath a conspicuously low severity line. Worked / didn't — compact, dense, still visually distinctive; not a chart type the average op-ed reader sees every week, so it earns its space the way the spiral did; the cost is that color is a coarser channel than position, so readers walk away with patterns rather than numbers. To carry into the final — this is the candidate to develop; open questions about per-capita normalization, the exact severity proxy, and color-scale calibration are deferred to the rationale in Section V.

IV · Final Design

Two Pandemics in One

V · Rationale & Reflection

Design rationale

Rationale & encoding choices

The redesign focuses on a single claim: the United States experienced two related but distinct pandemic patterns — one defined by case counts, and one by lethality — that peaked about a year apart. It uses a calendar heatmap with an added severity strip: years are arranged in three rows, and weeks in fifty-three columns, reserving position for the comparisons that matter most: when, and which year. Cell colour encodes case magnitude, where broad pattern is more important than precise lookup, while the case-fatality ratio is placed on the strip using vertical position, giving the article’s central scientific claim a stronger visual channel. Daily counts are aggregated to ISO weeks to reduce noise; the colour scale is square-root rather than linear because cases vary across several orders of magnitude; the CFR is computed with a two-week lag to reflect the delay between infection and death; and early 2020 is omitted from the strip because testing was too limited to support meaningful comparison. The colourmap also avoids red, since that would introduce an alarmist tone into a chart meant to support a more cautiously optimistic argument, and it is anchored to the page’s cream background so low-value cells recede. What the design loses is the spiral’s iconic lead-image quality. It is more of a reading chart than a seeing chart: it supports comparison more effectively, but does not produce the same immediate visual impact. The smaller 2020 waves may also appear less significant than they felt at the time, and the case-fatality ratio remains an imperfect proxy for severity.

Reflection: critique by redesign

The redesign substantially improves comparability: the question was Delta bigger than winter 2020–21?, which was difficult to answer from the spiral, becomes a comparison between two columns of differently saturated blue. It also somewhat improves narrative alignment, with a calmer overall form and a severity strip that better reflects Shaman’s actual claim. However, the redesign also surfaces a tension that the initial critique did not fully acknowledge: a single chart may not be able to preserve both the visceral force of an op-ed image and the clarity of analytical comparison. The spiral’s drama was doing real work on a Sunday opinion page; a scrolling reader might stop for the spiral in a way they may not for a calendar heatmap. The redesign chooses comparability, and accepts that loss. A more complete op-ed treatment might need both: the spiral as the visual lead, and the calendar heatmap as the explanatory body figure. The broader lesson is that pandemic data resists single-chart treatment because it tracks several phenomena at once, moving at different speeds. A fair critique should therefore specify which argument it is judging the design against; a redesign is not better in the abstract, only better for a particular argumentative task.
